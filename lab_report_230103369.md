## Phase 1 — False sharing and process scaling

The shared-memory experiment performed 16,000,000 logical update operations with 8 workers. Stride denotes the spacing, in 64-bit elements, between workers' designated counters. Each configuration used repeated runs summarized by median and interquartile range (IQR).

### Stride experiment (8 workers)

| Stride (uint64 elements) | Byte offset | Median (ms) | IQR (ms) | Throughput (Mops/s) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 8 | 549.225 | 14.780 | 29.132 |
| 2 | 16 | 577.618 | 25.094 | 27.700 |
| 4 | 32 | 573.255 | 9.917 | 27.911 |
| 8 | 64 | 693.056 | 74.060 | 23.086 |
| 16 | 128 | 638.875 | 65.566 | 25.044 |
| 32 | 256 | 627.269 | 44.689 | 25.507 |

### Scaling experiment

| Stride | Processes | Median (ms) | Speedup | Efficiency | Karp–Flatt e(p) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1 | 2317.574 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | — |
| 1 | 2 | 1237.120 | 1.8734 | 0.9367 | 0.0676 |
| 1 | 4 | 756.035 | 3.0654 | 0.7664 | 0.1016 |
| 1 | 8 | 549.225 | 4.2197 | 0.5275 | 0.1280 |
| 16 | 1 | 2369.617 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | — |
| 16 | 2 | 1288.674 | 1.8388 | 0.9194 | 0.0877 |
| 16 | 4 | 770.126 | 3.0769 | 0.7692 | 0.1000 |
| 16 | 8 | 638.875 | 3.7090 | 0.4636 | 0.1653 |

Definitions: `S(p) = T(1) / T(p)`; `E(p) = S(p) / p`; `e(p) = (1/S(p) - 1/p) / (1 - 1/p)` for `p > 1`.

**Interpretation.** Contrary to the usual false-sharing expectation, stride 16 (128-byte spacing) did **not** outperform stride 1: at 8 workers it took 638.875 ms versus 549.225 ms. Karp–Flatt at 8 workers was 0.1653 for stride 16 versus 0.1280 for stride 1. This run therefore does not establish a measurable false-sharing recovery. Python execution costs, process scheduling, heterogeneous M2 cores, and measurement variation may have obscured cache-coherence effects. No positive false-sharing result is claimed.

## Phase 2 — Strong scaling, weak scaling and scaling models

The benchmark had a serial Stage A (5% of the input, involving trigonometric and square-root work) followed by a parallel Stage B with 30 arithmetic/trigonometric operations per element. Worker pools were kept persistent across timed trials. Strong scaling used fixed `N = 1,200,000`; weak scaling used `N = 200,000 × p`.

### Strong scaling (`N = 1,200,000`)

| Processes | Median runtime (ms) | Speedup | Efficiency |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 956.010 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 |
| 2 | 501.517 | 1.9062 | 0.9531 |
| 3 | 351.993 | 2.7160 | 0.9053 |
| 4 | 287.799 | 3.3218 | 0.8304 |
| 5 | 274.758 | 3.4795 | 0.6959 |
| 6 | 264.531 | 3.6140 | 0.6023 |
| 7 | 244.003 | 3.9180 | 0.5597 |
| 8 | 232.453 | 4.1127 | 0.5141 |

Observed Stage A time at `p = 1`: **22.064 ms**. The measured Stage A fraction was **0.023080**, implying an **Amdahl asymptotic limit of approximately 43.3284×** under the simplified fixed-serial-fraction model. This is a model-derived upper bound, **not** an achieved speedup. The observed increase in speedup from 4 to 5 workers was approximately **0.1577**.

### Weak scaling (`N₀ = 200,000` elements per worker)

|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|

Weak efficiency is computed as `T(1, N₀) / T(p, pN₀)`. Gustafson's modeled speedup uses `G(p) = p - α(p)(p-1)`. The reported `α` values are experiment-specific and need not remain constant as workload grows.

These results indicate increasing overhead and/or resource contention as process count increases. The Gustafson figure **7.2092×** is a model estimate, not the measured strong-scaling speedup.

## Phase 3 — Empirical roofline and cache-blocked GEMM

The **final corrected run** is used here. Earlier Phase 3 measurements and the earlier hash are superseded and must not be submitted as final results.

### Effective memory and compute measurements

| Metric | Final observed value |
|---|---:|
| Memory copy size | Two buffers of 64,000,000 B each |
| Worker processes | 8 |
| Median copy time | 0.010405 s |
| Copy-time IQR | 0.013233 s |
| Effective bandwidth | 12.3014 GB/s |
| Scalar compute median | 0.891343 s |
| Compute-time IQR | 0.032453 s |
| Counted total compute work | 480,000,000 FLOPs |
| Python scalar compute throughput | 0.5385 GFLOP/s |
| Empirical ridge point | 0.0438 FLOP/byte |

Bandwidth was calculated from `2 × 64,000,000 bytes / measured seconds`, counting one read and one write. Workers were started before timing, so process creation was excluded from the copy interval. The compute test used 16 explicitly unrolled scalar multiply-add expressions per iteration and 60 million counted FLOPs per worker. These Python expressions do not guarantee fused hardware FMA instructions.

The ridge point was computed as `0.5385 GFLOP/s ÷ 12.3014 GB/s ≈ 0.0438 FLOP/byte` using the measured Python-level ceilings. The bandwidth IQR (**13.233 ms**) exceeds its median (**10.405 ms**), showing substantial run-to-run variability; this result should not be described as a stable DRAM hardware peak.

### GEMM (`N = 256`, double precision)

Performance uses `2N³ / runtime` FLOPs.

| Variant | Block size | Median time (s) | GFLOP/s | Max absolute error | Estimated tile bytes | Nominal intensity (FLOP/B) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Naive `ijk` | — | 0.4768 | **0.0704** | — | — | — |
| Reordered `ikj` | — | 0.5412 | 0.0620 | — | — | — |
| Blocked | 8 | 0.5947 | 0.0564 | 0.000e+00 | 1,536 | 0.6667 |
| Blocked | 16 | 0.5806 | 0.0578 | 0.000e+00 | 6,144 | 1.3333 |
| Blocked | 32 | 0.5757 | 0.0583 | 0.000e+00 | 24,576 | 2.6667 |
| Blocked | 64 | 0.6080 | 0.0552 | 0.000e+00 | 98,304 | 5.3333 |
| Blocked | 96 | 0.5656 | 0.0593 | 0.000e+00 | 221,184 | 8.0000 |
| Blocked | 128 | 0.5558 | **0.0604** | 0.000e+00 | 393,216 | 10.6667 |

All blocked outputs matched the reordered reference with a reported maximum absolute difference of zero, satisfying the `< 1e-9` threshold. The program did not separately report the difference between the naive and reordered outputs.

The best **blocked** tile size was **128**, but the fastest algorithm overall was **naive `ijk`** at **0.0704 GFLOP/s**. The BS=128 three-tile estimate (`3 × 128² × 8 = 393,216 B`, or 384 KiB) exceeds the reported 64 KiB L1d capacity but fits within the reported 4 MiB L2 capacity. These footprint and arithmetic-intensity calculations are approximations, not direct cache-miss measurements.

**Interpretation.** Blocking did not outperform the naive loop in this Python implementation. Interpreter overhead, repeated indexing and loop nesting can outweigh theoretical cache-locality gains. The observed roofline is an **empirical Python-level roofline**, not an estimate of the Apple M2's hardware peak.

## Phase 4 — Scheduling and load imbalance

The experiment used **8,000** indexed tasks and **8** worker processes. Work increased with index according to the implemented integer expression `K(i) = 1 + (i // 40)²`, with repeated `math.sin` evaluations. Three scheduling methods were tested: static contiguous blocks, static cyclic distribution, and dynamic work claiming with chunk sizes `1, 8, 64, 512`. Each configuration used one warmup and five measured runs.

| Scheduling strategy | Chunk size | Median wall time (ms) | IQR (ms) | Imbalance λ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Static block | — | 2419.673 | 40.949 | 2.4841 |
| Static cyclic | — | 1695.356 | 56.050 | 1.0524 |
| **Dynamic** | **1** | **1424.814** | 33.902 | **1.0030** |
| Dynamic | 8 | 1505.104 | 30.318 | 1.0130 |
| Dynamic | 64 | 1531.237 | 16.361 | 1.0867 |
| Dynamic | 512 | 1878.021 | 23.891 | 1.4698 |

Imbalance is defined as `λ = max(worker elapsed time) / mean(worker elapsed time)`. The measured worker interval includes scheduling and synchronization inside each worker, not only arithmetic. Checksums were compared across all configurations within a specified numerical tolerance; no mismatch was reported.

**Best dynamic chunk:** `C = 1`.

**Speedup over static block:** `2419.673 / 1424.814 ≈ 1.6982×`, equivalent to approximately **41.1% less wall time**.

**Interpretation.** Static block scheduling concentrated expensive high-index work in later workers and showed the largest imbalance (`λ = 2.4841`). Static cyclic distribution improved balance (`λ = 1.0524`). Dynamic scheduling with the smallest chunk delivered the lowest median time and a nearly balanced worker interval (`λ = 1.0030`). Increasing the chunk to 512 reduced the scheduler's ability to redistribute heavy work, raising imbalance to `1.4698`. The best chunk size is specific to this workload and machine.

**Evidence:** `evidence/phase4_scheduling.csv`, `evidence/phase4_worker_times.csv`.

## Limitations and reproducibility

1. **Hardware heterogeneity:** Apple M2 has performance and efficiency cores; eight Python processes need not receive identical compute resources.
2. **False sharing:** The Phase 1 stride results do not demonstrate the expected recovery from padding. This negative result is preserved rather than replaced by an expected or simulated value.
3. **Roofline methodology:** Phase 3 is dominated by Python-level implementation characteristics and has high bandwidth timing variability. It is not an architectural peak Roofline measurement.
4. **GEMM precision:** The reported zero errors compare blocked output with the reordered reference, not with an independent high-precision oracle.
5. **Scheduling measurements:** Phase 4 worker active intervals include scheduler overhead; `λ` is an operational balance indicator rather than pure useful-compute utilization.
6. **Source provenance:** This report was compiled from actual observed terminal outputs. To reproduce the measurements, retain and submit the original `harness.py`, `task0_cal.py`, `task1_falsesharing.py`, `task2_scaling.py`, `task3_roofline_gemm.py`, `task4_scheduling.py` and their `evidence/` CSVs. Source code is deliberately **not reconstructed or invented** in this report.

## Conclusion

The lab illustrates that increasing process count alone does not guarantee linear speedup. Strong scaling reached 4.1127× at eight workers, but efficiency declined as concurrency increased. The false-sharing benchmark did not show the anticipated padded-counter advantage. In GEMM, the theoretically cache-friendlier blocked variants did not beat naive multiplication under Python interpreter overhead. Conversely, scheduling strategy made a substantial measurable difference: dynamic chunk size 1 reduced wall time by approximately 41.1% compared with static contiguous assignment. The conclusions are limited to the actual tested implementations and recorded runs.
